Nobody's plan fails at a home-cooked lunch. It fails on the fourth delivery order of the week, or on a Sunday lunch where the table was full of good food and the plate was refilled twice.

The useful response to that is not "eat out less". Eating out is part of a normal life, and a plan that cannot survive a restaurant is not a plan. What actually helps is knowing the three things that change when someone else cooks, and having a default order you do not have to think about.

## What changes when someone else cooks

1. **Fat is invisible again.** A restaurant finishes a dish with oil or butter for shine, thickens a gravy with cream, and fries things you would have cooked on a tawa. You cannot see any of it, and it is the largest single difference between a restaurant dish and the same dish at home.
2. **The portion is the kitchen's decision, not yours.** What arrives is a serving for one hungry person, which is often more than you would have put on a plate yourself.
3. **The meal arrives with company.** Breads, a starter, a drink, a dessert. Each is a separate decision that nobody made deliberately, and together they are bigger than the main dish.

The good news is that all three are decided at the moment of ordering, in about ninety seconds, which is the only part of eating out anybody actually controls.

## What to order

Think in terms of how the dish was cooked, not whether it is "Indian" or "healthy".

**Usually a good order**

- **Tandoori or tikka anything** — chicken, paneer, fish, mushroom. Dry heat, no sauce to absorb, and protein comes in a recognisable portion.
- **A dry sabzi, a chana or rajma dish, a dal without cream.** Restaurant dal is often better than homemade because of the butter, but plain dal is still one of the safest things on any menu.
- **Roti instead of naan.** A restaurant naan is usually two to three times the dough of a roti, and a butter naan adds a visible spoonful of butter on top of that.
- **Raita, salad, chaas.** The cold things that make the meal feel finished, and the cheapest fibre on the table.
- **Idli, plain dosa, steamed momos, grilled or tawa items** where the cuisine is South Indian or East Asian.
- **Water first**, then decide if you want anything else. Most of what gets ordered is ordered thirsty.

**Depends on the portion**

- **Biryani.** It is rice and protein and a lot of oil, which is fine in a katori portion and different in a full plate. Serve it out rather than eating from the bowl.
- **Any curry with a tomato-onion base, no cream.** Better than the creamy version, still served with a ladle of oil on top.
- **Anything with rice or bread as a course of its own** — that is the part that stacks up quietly.

**Worth asking yourself about**

- Butter, malai, cream, shahi, korma, makhani: the words on the menu are telling you what is in the dish.
- Fried starters, which are the easiest thing to eat while waiting and the hardest to account for afterwards.
- Naan, especially butter naan, and extra breads generally.
- Sweet lassi, cold drinks and mocktails — a sweet lassi is closer to a dessert than to a drink.
- Dessert at the end, when nobody is hungry enough to enjoy it properly.

## The four habits that do the most work

- **One dry, one gravy, shared.** Two dishes between two people, not four. You end up eating more protein, more vegetables and less sauce, and the bill is smaller.
- **Skip the starter, or make it the table's.** The waiting-time food is where meals quietly double.
- **Decide about dessert before the food arrives.** If the answer is yes, order one and share — a decision made at the start survives the end of the meal better than a decision made when someone else's plate appears.
- **Do not arrive starving.** A katori of curd or a handful of chana an hour before is the cheapest way to stop ordering the whole menu.

## Delivery is a different problem

Ordering in removes the social friction, and adds two of its own: no one is watching the portions, and the food is designed to be satisfying at a distance — which means more oil, more salt and a bigger container.

Three things we do:

1. **Split it before eating.** Serve a portion onto a plate and put the rest away. What arrives usually holds two meals, and eating from the container means eating all of it.
2. **Order the main, add the rest at home.** One delivered dish plus rice or roti cooked at home, plus curd and a salad, is a genuinely good meal for two people and costs less than two orders.
3. **Choose the place by what it cooks well.** A kitchen that makes a good tandoori or a decent dal is a better habit than one that makes the best butter chicken in the city.

## How to log a meal you did not cook

The same way you log a home-cooked dish, with one adjustment: assume more fat than you would have used. [Logging components rather than dish names](/blog/why-home-cooked-food-is-hard-to-track) works here too — "one katori of chicken curry, two rotis, raita" is loggable, and no app on earth can tell you what the restaurant actually put in the pan.

The honest version is that the number will be a band, not a value, and the band is wide. That is fine. The point of logging a meal out is not precision, it is finding out whether the week still works with two of them in it — which is a question about frequency, not about an exact figure.

## What this post is not saying

It is not saying that restaurant food is bad, that you should feel guilty about a Sunday lunch, or that food eaten with people you like is purely a calorie transaction. It is also not written for the case where eating out is most of the week — if you travel for work or live alone without a kitchen, "order better" is not the answer; the answer is a rotation of three or four places and dishes you trust, and getting some food cooked at home on the days you can.

The frequency is the lever, and [the rest of the plan does not change](/blog/weight-loss-with-indian-food-without-banning-rice) — that post is also where the arithmetic of [a week that did not actually change](/blog/eating-healthy-not-losing-weight) starts. If the meal included a sweet drink and a dessert, the sugar arithmetic is [in the post on an ordinary day](/blog/sugar-in-an-ordinary-indian-day). And if you want the plate logic to apply to a restaurant table, it is the same four jobs as [here](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali) — protein, vegetables, a measured grain, something cold at the side.
