Most people asking about protein are really asking one of two things: *am I getting enough*, and *how would I know?* Both are answerable without a nutrition degree, and without turning dinner into a spreadsheet. What follows is the reasoning we use when we cook, plus the numbers we rely on when we read a recipe back.

One thing before anything else: nothing here is medical advice. If you have a kidney condition, are pregnant, or take medication that affects how you process protein, the person to ask is your doctor, not a food blog.

## Start from your body weight, not from a chart

Indian dietary guidelines put adult protein needs at roughly **0.8 to 1 gram per kilogram of body weight per day** for a healthy adult. That is the maintenance figure — enough to replace what your body turns over, not enough to build anything new.

So the arithmetic is short:

- 55 kg → about 44 to 55 g a day
- 70 kg → about 56 to 70 g a day
- 85 kg → about 68 to 85 g a day

Two situations push the number up, and both are common:

- **Losing weight.** In a calorie deficit your body is more likely to draw on muscle, so the guidance most dietitians work from is closer to **1.0 to 1.2 g per kg**, and higher protein is also what keeps you full between meals.
- **Lifting, or over 60.** Muscle responds to protein more slowly as you age, and training increases the demand. Again, closer to 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg.

If you are somewhere in the middle of ordinary life — no deficit, no training plan — the 0.8 to 1 figure is the one to use.

## What 60 grams looks like in an Indian kitchen

This is where most people are surprised, and where we spend most of our time in the kitchen. Protein per portion, approximate, cooked as you would actually eat it:

| Food | Usual portion | Protein |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cooked dal | 1 katori, about 150 g | 7 to 9 g |
| Rajma or chana | 1 katori, cooked | 7 to 9 g |
| Curd | 1 katori, about 150 g | 5 to 6 g |
| Milk | 1 glass, 250 ml | 8 g |
| Paneer | 100 g | 18 to 20 g |
| Whole egg | 1 large | 6 g |
| Soya chunks | 30 g dry, about 1 katori soaked | 15 g |
| Chicken breast | 100 g cooked | about 30 g |
| Fish, such as bangda | 100 g cooked | 19 to 22 g |
| Roti | 1 medium, 30 g atta | about 3 g |
| Cooked rice | 1 cup, about 150 g | 3 to 4 g |
| Moong or mixed sprouts | 150 g | 5 to 7 g |

Read that table once more, and the usual problem becomes visible. A plate of two rotis, a katori of dal, a bowl of rice and a sabzi lands somewhere around **20 to 24 g of protein**. For a 70 kg adult that is not a bad meal — but if the other two meals look the same, the day finishes at 60 g only by accident.

We round deliberately. Portion sizes vary wildly between households, brands differ, and a dal that has been simmered down carries more per spoon than a thin one. Treat each figure as a band, not a promise.

## The two mistakes that keep the number low

**Protein only at dinner.** Almost every low-protein day we have measured has the same shape: tea and biscuits at 8, a light lunch, then a large dinner doing all the work. The body does not store protein for later the way it stores fat, and a very large single dose is used less efficiently than the same total spread across the day. Moving one protein source to breakfast is the single highest-value change available, and it costs nothing.

**Protein that arrives with oil.** Two samosas have protein in them. So does a plate of pakoras. They also arrive with a large amount of oil, which is why "I eat protein" and "I am eating better" can both be true and still not add up to progress. ## If most of your meals are vegetarian

Vegetarian Indian food can absolutely hit the target; it just needs the protein treated as the starting point rather than the afterthought. Four things do most of the work in our kitchen:

- **Soya chunks.** Gram for gram the cheapest high-protein ingredient available, and it takes on whatever you cook it in.
- **Paneer and curd.** Both are complete proteins and both appear in dishes people already like.
- **A pulse at every meal, in a real portion.** A katori of dal at lunch and a katori at dinner is 16 to 18 g on its own.
- **Combining, without over-thinking it.** Dal with rice, curd with roti, chana with anything — Indian meals have been combining grains and pulses for a very long time.

You do not have to combine perfectly at every meal. Over a day, a mixed vegetarian diet covers what it needs. What it will not do is cover it by chance at 20 g per plate.

## How to know whether it is working

Weighing food forever is not realistic, and we would not suggest it. What does work, in our experience:

1. **Estimate one ordinary day, once.** Use the table above, add it up, and see whether you are near your own target. Most people find the answer in one attempt.
2. **Fix the weakest meal rather than every meal.** One change held for a month beats five changes held for four days.
3. **Watch the trend, not the day.** Weight moves with water, salt and sleep; a single reading means very little, a month of them means a lot.

## What we are not saying

We are not saying everyone needs a protein supplement, a specific gram figure, or protein at every single meal. Most Indian households get closer than they think by making two deliberate choices rather than fifteen. And we are not going to quote an exact number as though your body came with one printed on it: the ranges above are what the guidance says, and your own requirement sits inside them somewhere.
