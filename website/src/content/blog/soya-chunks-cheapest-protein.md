Soya chunks are the cheapest serious protein available in an Indian kitchen, and most people who buy a packet of them cook them once, find them spongy and tasteless, and leave the rest at the back of the shelf. That reaction is fair. The problem is almost never the soya.

Here is what they are, what they are worth, and the two things that decide whether you will actually eat them.

## What they are, and what they are worth

Soya chunks — also sold as soya badi, meal maker or textured soy protein — are defatted soy flour, extruded and dried. That is worth saying plainly: this is a processed food, not a bean. It is not the same food as boiled soybeans or edamame, and nothing here is claiming otherwise.

What it is, is concentrated:

| Per 100 g as sold | Protein | Calories | Fibre | Fat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soya chunks (dry) | about 52 g | about 330 | about 13 g | about 1 g |
| Paneer | about 18 g | about 265 | none | about 20 g |
| Chana or rajma (dry) | about 20 to 24 g | about 350 | about 12 g | about 2 g |
| Toor dal (dry) | about 22 g | about 340 | about 13 g | about 2 g |
| Chicken breast | about 31 g | about 165 | none | about 4 g |

A useful column that food tables rarely print is **protein per 100 calories**, because that is what decides whether a food is "high protein" in practice:

| | Protein per 100 kcal |
| --- | --- |
| Soya chunks | about 16 g |
| Eggs | about 9 g |
| Paneer | about 7 g |
| Chana, dal | about 6 g |
| Chicken breast | about 19 g |
| Whey | about 20 g |

Put another way: soya lands just below chicken and whey and far above every vegetarian staple on this page. A 30 g handful — a small fistful — carries about 15 g of protein. A 50 g portion, which is what a main dish for one person takes, carries about 26 g.

## Price, briefly

They are cheaper per gram of protein than paneer, eggs, chicken, dals and whey, and that ordering has been stable for years. We are not printing rupee figures, because prices move by city, brand and month, and a number written into a post is wrong within a season. If you want to check it yourself, divide the packet price by the grams of protein on the label — it takes a minute and the answer is not close.

## Why they taste of nothing

Because they are designed to. The extrusion process leaves a neutral, spongy, high-surface-area piece precisely so it can absorb whatever it is cooked in. That is the feature. The failure mode is cooking them like a vegetable and expecting them to carry the dish.

Two things decide the outcome, and both are about water:

1. **Soak them properly.** Hot water, ten to fifteen minutes, until they are completely soft through the middle. A pinch of salt in the water helps.
2. **Then squeeze them.** This is the step people skip and it is the one that matters. Squeeze each piece like a sponge until no more water comes out, then squeeze again. Soya that goes into a pan still holding its soaking water will stay tasteless no matter what you put with it. Some kitchens go further and dry-roast or air-fry the squeezed pieces for two minutes so they firm up instead of dissolving into the gravy.

## The method that works

- **Cook them in something wet and spiced.** Onion, tomato, ginger, garlic, chilli — a base with a sauce in it, so the piece has something to absorb. Dry masala tossed with soya stays bland.
- **Or grind them.** Soak, squeeze, and pulse to a coarse mince. Now they behave like keema: they bind with an egg or with oats, take a crust on a tawa, and stop being recognisable as soya at all. This is the trick that turns soya chunks from a chore into a fast breakfast, and it is exactly what the cheela below does.
- **Keep the oil out of the equation.** The one place soya is worse than paneer is its dryness, which tempts people into frying it. A non-stick pan, a wet base and a squeeze of lemon does more for it than oil, and [oil is the easiest calorie in the kitchen to spend without noticing](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka) — [the pan-by-pan version](/blog/cooking-without-oil-pan-by-pan) has what that means for each dish.

## The rumour, honestly

The claim that soya affects men's hormones is the reason half the people who ask us about soya ask. Here is the version we can stand behind:

- Soya contains isoflavones, which are plant compounds that can interact weakly with hormone receptors. That is where the scare comes from.
- Human studies at normal food-level intakes have not shown feminising effects in men — the striking findings come from animal studies at doses far above what food provides.
- If you would rather not eat soya on that basis, that is a choice you are entitled to make. But it is worth knowing that the evidence is not what is being implied, and that the alternative at this price per gram of protein is usually nothing at all.
- **Thyroid medication is the exception worth taking seriously.** Soy can interfere with how levothyroxine is absorbed, and timing matters. If you take thyroid medication, ask your doctor or pharmacist about the gap between the two rather than settling it from a blog.
- **And soya is a common allergen.** If you react to it, none of this applies.

There is also the honest limitation that no one likes to print: it will never taste like chicken. It tastes like whatever you cooked it in, which is either the reason to use it or the reason not to.

## How it fits a week

We use them two or three times a week, mostly because rotation is what keeps a kitchen interesting rather than because there is a limit to hit. If the question you arrived with is which of the four kitchen proteins to lean on rather than what to do with soya, [this comparison answers it by the serving](/blog/paneer-eggs-dal-soya-which-protein). A 50 g portion in a main dish carries about 26 g of protein, so a meal built on soya plus a katori of dal and a chapati is comfortably over 35 g — the arithmetic that most Indian plates are short on, which is [the protein question this whole series starts from](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet).

They also carry fibre, about 6 g in a 50 g portion, which is unusual for a protein: chicken and paneer bring none. That makes them one of the few additions that help both numbers at once — worth knowing if [the fibre side of the day](/blog/fibre-in-an-indian-diet) is where yours falls short.
