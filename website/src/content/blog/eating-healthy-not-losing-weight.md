This is the quietest way for a change to fail. Nobody binges, nobody gives up — the cooking genuinely improves, the vegetables go up, the fried things go down, and two months later the number on the scale has moved by a kilogram in either direction. It is more common than the crash, and it is harder to fix, because there is nothing obvious to blame.

When we read a food log in that shape, the explanation is almost always one of four things. They are worth checking in this order, because the first is the most common and the last is the most annoying.

## 1. The quantity did not change

"Healthy" describes food, not portions. Dal, roti, rice and sabzi can be cooked beautifully and still add up to the same energy as before — the plate got better and the amount stayed identical, which is exactly the failure mode where effort goes in and nothing comes out.

The change that works here is smaller than people expect and happens at the serving spoon rather than in the kitchen: **two fists of rice to one**, decided when you serve rather than when you cook. Rice is about 130 calories a katori, so that single decision is roughly 130 calories a day, and unlike cutting rice out entirely, it is one you can keep. We argue the whole case [in the rice post](/blog/weight-loss-with-indian-food-without-banning-rice).

Worth saying plainly: if nobody is measuring anything, "the quantity did not change" is a guess. [Estimating home cooking](/blog/why-home-cooked-food-is-hard-to-track) is written for exactly this — three numbers, roughly, rather than a spreadsheet.

## 2. The oil

This is the one that catches people who have already done the obvious work. An ordinary Indian kitchen spends **around 300 calories a day on oil before anything has been fried on purpose** — the sabzi, the tadka, the tawa, a snack — and none of it feels like a decision. A "healthy" day that swapped refined flour for millet and kept the same four spoons of oil is a day that changed its ingredients and not its arithmetic.

Halving it is about 150 calories, which in our experience is more than most people achieve by giving up rice entirely. [The post that measures it](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka) also has the two techniques that do the work without changing how the food tastes, and [the pan-by-pan version](/blog/cooking-without-oil-pan-by-pan) says which dishes this does not apply to.

## 3. The hour between five and eight, and the cup

Two items, one reason: they are the places calories are spent without being noticed as food.

- **Chai and coffee.** Two teaspoons a cup, four times a day, is 32 g of sugar — the largest single line in most Indian days, and the easiest one to change. [That post has the arithmetic](/blog/sugar-in-an-ordinary-indian-day).
- **The evening.** Not a big meal; a packet, twice, standing up. The fix is not discipline at 5 pm, it is [having something that is ready](/blog/high-protein-evening-snacks) — a katori of chana, curd, two boiled eggs.

These two are worth checking before dinner is touched, because they are the changes that do not require anyone else in the house to eat differently.

## 4. The week, not the day

A good Monday to Friday and a large Saturday is a week that did not change. The restaurant meal, the takeaway, the family dinner — none of them is a problem on its own, and all of them together can be the whole difference. [Eating out without losing the plan](/blog/eating-out-without-losing-the-plan) is about that set of decisions specifically.

The measurement side matters here too. Weigh yourself in the same conditions, once or twice a week, and read the **month**. Salt, sleep and hormones move that number by a kilogram either way, which is larger than two weeks of real progress — so a bad Tuesday tells you nothing and a flat fortnight tells you very little. A tape measure at the waist is less noisy than the scale. Rates commonly given as safe and sustainable for most adults sit around a quarter to half a kilogram a week, and if you have only a few kilograms to lose, expect slower than that and expect it to look like nothing for two or three weeks at a time.

## What to do with all this

Pick **one** row, change it for two weeks, and look at the month rather than the scale. In our reading of logs, the order that works is the order above: portion first, oil second, then the cup and the evening, and only then the grain or the dinner itself.

Adding protein is a separate lever, and a useful one, because it is what keeps an afternoon from being settled by biscuits — but it makes a deficit tolerable rather than creating one. [How much protein you need](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet) is where to start on that side.

## What we do not claim

This is not a plan, and there is no promised rate of loss in it. Nothing here is medical advice, and if you have diabetes, thyroid issues, PCOS, are pregnant, or take medication, that conversation belongs with your doctor before it belongs with a page about food.

One more honest limit: **if the arithmetic genuinely does not add up** — the food is measured, the change is real, and nothing is moving over a couple of months — then the answer is a conversation with a doctor rather than a stricter version of the same diet. We are not going to diagnose that from a blog post, and neither should anyone else.
