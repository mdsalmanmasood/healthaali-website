A balanced plate is a boring idea that most people have never actually assembled. Ask someone to describe their lunch and you get a list of dishes, not a plate: roti, dal, sabzi, rice, maybe curd. Ask what job each of those was doing and the answer is usually "that is just what we eat".

That is not a criticism. It is the reason the same plate can be 400 calories or 900, and the reason two people eating "the same food" get very different results. So here is the version we cook from: four jobs, checked by hand, repeated until it stops needing thought.

## The four jobs

1. **A protein.** Something that is deliberate about it — dal, chana, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, chicken, soya. Not "there is some besan in the bhaji".
2. **Vegetables, in a real quantity.** A katori of sabzi and a handful of raw salad at the side. This is where most of the fibre and micronutrients come from.
3. **A grain.** Rice or roti, in a portion you decided rather than a portion that happened.
4. **Something fermented or cold at the side.** Curd, buttermilk, a pickle in small quantity, a chaas. It is what makes a plate feel finished.

Four jobs, four things. A plate missing one of them is not a small version of a balanced plate; it is a different plate, and it usually shows up as hunger an hour later.

## Judge the portions with your hand

Nobody weighs food at lunch. You do have your own hands, and they scale with you, which is why the guidance has lasted so long:

- **Protein: a palm.** Roughly 100 g cooked fish, chicken or paneer, or one to two katoris of cooked dal, chana or rajma.
- **Grain: a fist.** About a cup of cooked rice, or two rotis.
- **Vegetables: two cupped hands.** More than most plates carry.
- **Fat: a thumb.** One teaspoon, if you are counting the oil you cook in.

None of this is precise, and it does not need to be. It is precise enough to tell the difference between a plate with 20 g of protein and one with 40 g, which is the difference that actually changes how the afternoon goes.

## A worked plate

This is a real lunch from our channel. Here is how the four jobs are filled on it:

| On the plate | The job it does |
| --- | --- |
| Zero-oil mutton liver pepper fry | Protein, and a fair amount of iron and B12 |
| Two whole-wheat rotis | The grain, plus some fibre |
| Moong salad | Plant protein and more fibre |
| Buttermilk | The cold, fermented side |

The total we measured for the video is **about 490 calories, about 40 g of protein and roughly 9 to 10 g of fibre**. We are giving the plate total and not a number per item, because per-item numbers for a home-cooked dish are an estimate dressed up as a measurement — the plate is recognisably an Indian lunch, and it hits those totals because all four jobs are filled.

## The one change that makes a plate lighter

If a plate is running heavy and you do not want to eat less, look at the oil before you look at the rice. Two tablespoons of oil is 240 calories and adds no volume, no fibre and no protein — it is the single largest source of calories that does not make you feel fed. Rice, by comparison, is about 130 calories a katori and comes with the meal.

We wrote a whole post on that: [where the oil actually hides](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka). If you only read one of the two, read that one.

But rice is not free either, and we are not pretending otherwise. The honest version is that a fist of rice fits most plates comfortably, and two fists usually does not. Decide which one you are having rather than letting the serving spoon decide.

## When it stops working

A balanced plate is not a fix for everything. If you are training hard, pregnant, managing a condition, or simply not losing weight where you expected to, the plate is the wrong place to look for the answer — that is a conversation with your doctor or a dietitian.

What a balanced plate does do, reliably, is make the estimate easy. When protein, vegetables, grain and a side are all present and all judged in portions you can see, there is not much left to track, because there is not much left to guess.
