Ask ten people in an Indian kitchen where their protein comes from and nine will say dal. Ask how much, and the answers will range from "lots" to "I think it is enough". Both answers can be true of the same katori, because dal is one of the few foods where the number depends almost entirely on how you cooked it rather than on what you bought.

So here is the arithmetic, in the order it actually gets asked.

## The number on the packet is not the number in the bowl

Dry pulses are about a quarter protein by weight. That number is stable and well established:

| Pulse (100 g dry) | Protein | Fibre |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toor / arhar dal | about 22 g | about 13 g |
| Moong (whole or dal) | about 24 g | about 12 g |
| Chana dal | about 20 g | about 12 g |
| Rajma | about 24 g | about 14 g |
| Urad dal | about 24 g | about 12 g |
| Kabuli chana | about 20 g | about 12 g |

Food composition tables differ by a gram or two here, which is not the part that matters. What matters is what happens next: dal absorbs roughly two and a half to three times its own weight in water while it cooks. Nothing is lost, but everything is diluted. A hundred grams of dry toor dal weighs about 270 g in the pot, and the protein is now spread across all of it.

## What one katori carries

This is where the 7 g and the 15 g katoris come from, and neither is a mistake:

| On the plate | Protein | Fibre |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 katori (150 ml) thick dal, cooked from about 60 g dry | about 12 to 13 g | about 7 g |
| 1 katori of the same dal cooked thinner, from about 40 g dry | about 8 to 9 g | about 5 g |
| 1 katori of rajma or chana | about 9 g | about 6 g |
| 2 whole-wheat rotis (about 60 g flour) | about 6 g | about 6 g |

If your dal is the consistency of a soup, you have more servings in the same pot and less protein in each one. If it is thick enough to stand a spoon in, you have fewer servings and more protein per katori. Both are correct cooking. The katori is not a unit, which is why [estimating home food](/blog/why-home-cooked-food-is-hard-to-track) is about components rather than dish names.

If you have read [the protein post](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet), its table gives a katori of dal as 7 to 9 g, which is the thinner end of the range above. Both numbers are right. The water is the difference, and the water is a choice you make every time you cook.

## Why dal and roti are worth more together

Dal is short on one amino acid that wheat has plenty of, and wheat is short on one that dal has plenty of. Eaten together — which is what a thaali already does — the pair covers each other, and that combination has been the backbone of Indian protein intake for a very long time.

Two honest qualifications, because this fact gets stretched:

- **It matters most when the pulse is doing the work.** If dal and roti are your protein, the combination is doing real work. If the day also has curd, eggs, paneer, fish or chicken in it, the pairing is a nice detail rather than something to manage.
- **Total protein still comes first.** Combining foods changes how well the amino acids match; it does not create protein that was not in the pot. Two katoris of dal beat one katori of dal paired perfectly with anything.

## A pulse is a dish, not a garnish

The other half of the answer is quantity, and it is the half nobody discusses. A katori of dal at lunch and a katori at dinner is about 18 g of protein from pulses across the whole day. That is a real contribution and it is also short of what most adults need, which is the arithmetic in [how much protein you actually need on an Indian diet](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet).

The fix is not exotic. It is usually one of these:

1. **Make it thicker, not bigger.** The same katori, less water: three or four extra grams of protein and no extra cooking.
2. **Two pulses instead of one.** Dal plus a katori of chana or rajma turns 9 g into 18 g, and makes the meal more filling at the same time.
3. **Add a spoonful of something concentrated.** A tablespoon of besan stirred in, or a small handful of soya granules, adds 3 to 6 g to a pot that already exists.
4. **Put curd next to it.** Five to six grams, cold, and it is the side that makes the plate feel finished.

What does not work is adding ghee or a second tadka. Fat is not protein, and it is the easiest thing in the dish to spend without noticing — we measured that in [where the oil actually hides](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka).

## When dal is not enough

Pulses are a good answer to a moderate protein target and a poor answer to a high one. If you are aiming at 100 g a day, you are looking at six or seven katoris of thick dal, which nobody eats. That is why anyone training seriously ends up adding eggs, paneer, soya, fish or chicken, and it is not a failure of the dal.

One more limit worth stating plainly: if you have kidney disease, gout, or a condition that changes how much protein or purine you should eat, the useful number is the one your doctor gives you, not the one in this table. A general post about pulses is the wrong place to get that answer.

## What to take from this

A katori of dal is roughly 9 g of protein and roughly 5 g of fibre, and both numbers move when you change the water rather than the pulse. Dal with roti is better than either alone for reasons that are real but modest. Behind all of it, the count is still the count: if you want to know whether the day is working, [counting the whole plate](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali) tells you more than counting the dal.

Fibre deserves its own post, since the pulse in this one is already carrying a third of a day's worth — that is [where an Indian day usually falls short](/blog/fibre-in-an-indian-diet).
