Nearly every Indian kitchen has four serious proteins within reach: paneer in the fridge, eggs on the shelf, dal in the jar, soya in the cupboard. The question we get asked is which one to lean on — and it is a good question, because the four of them behave completely differently in the twenty minutes before dinner.

What follows is the comparison by **the serving you would actually eat**, which is the only version of the arithmetic that changes what you cook. The per-100 g tables are [in the soya post](/blog/soya-chunks-cheapest-protein) and the pulse-by-pulse ones are [in the dal post](/blog/how-much-protein-in-dal); both are worth reading, and neither answers this question.

## The serving, not the label

| What one serving looks like | Protein | Fibre | What it asks of you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 g paneer, cubed | about 18 g | none | nothing — it is safe to eat as it is |
| 2 eggs | about 12 g | none | six minutes, one pan |
| 1 katori (150 ml) thick dal | about 12 to 13 g | about 7 g | the pot you were already making |
| 1 katori of rajma or chana | about 9 g | about 6 g | a batch cooked on the weekend |
| 30 g dry soya chunks | about 15 g | about 4 g | ten to fifteen minutes of soaking, then a squeeze |
| 1 katori of curd | about 5 to 6 g | none | nothing |
| 2 whole-wheat rotis | about 6 g | about 6 g | the tawa, but you were doing that anyway |

Read down the "protein" column and the differences look modest; read down the last column and they stop looking modest at all. **The food you eat is the one that is ready when you are hungry**, which is why a kitchen with a packet of soya and no soaked soya behaves exactly like a kitchen with no soya in it.

## Four questions that decide it, in order

1. **Is it already cooking?** Then it wins. Dal that is already on the stove is 12 g of protein that costs you nothing but a ladle, and no amount of optimisation beats zero added effort.
2. **Is it ready in the fridge?** Paneer, curd, boiled eggs and a box of cooked chana turn a plate of roti and sabzi into a high-protein meal without a second dish. This is the single change that moves most Indian dinners, and it is a Sunday decision rather than a Wednesday one.
3. **Does it need the pan?** Eggs and soya both do, and both are quick. Soya is the one with a step people skip — [the squeeze](/blog/soya-chunks-cheapest-protein) — and skipping it is what makes soya taste like nothing.
4. **What does it cost per gram?** The ordering has been stable for years: soya is the cheapest, then eggs and pulses, then chicken, then paneer, and whey last. We do not print rupee figures, for the reason the soya post explains — a price written into a post is wrong within a season, and packet protein is on the label.

## "Which is best" is the wrong question

Three things get asked in this shape, and only one of them matters.

- **Complete versus incomplete.** Dal and roti cover each other's gaps, which is what a thaali has always done. The honest qualification is that combining foods does not create protein that was not in the pot: [two katoris beat one katori paired perfectly](/blog/how-much-protein-in-dal).
- **Speed.** Eggs, then paneer, then soya, then pulses. If the choice is between a fast protein and no protein at 9 pm, speed is not a compromise.
- **Total.** The only number that decides whether a day works is the whole plate, and it is [the one Indian home cooking most often misses](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet). A katori of dal at lunch and another at dinner is about 18 g; a day built on that alone is short, and no choice between these four foods fixes a total that is too low.

So the useful answer is not one food. It is **one of these at every meal**, chosen by what the hour allows: dal because it is already cooking, paneer or curd because they are in the fridge, eggs or soya because they are fast.

## What we do not claim

This page compares portions, not health outcomes. It is not a plan, and it is not advice about a condition: **if you have kidney disease, gout, or anything else that changes how much protein or purine you should eat, the number that matters is the one your doctor gives you**, not the one in a table here.

Two of the figures are ranges rather than numbers, and honestly so. A katori is not a unit — a thick dal and a watery one differ by half, and [the water is a choice you make every time you cook](/blog/why-home-cooked-food-is-hard-to-track). And the soya figure is a dry weight: 30 g of dry soya is not 30 g of what lands on the plate.
