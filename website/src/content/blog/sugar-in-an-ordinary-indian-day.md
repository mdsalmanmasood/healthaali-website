Nobody thinks of chai as food. That is the whole problem with sugar in an Indian day: it arrives in things that are not meals — a cup, a biscuit with the cup, a spoon in the curd, a sauce — and by the time it is in a dessert it is the smallest part of the total.

This is not an argument that sugar is poison. It is an argument that a number you have never counted is hard to make a decision about, and that the label on the front of a packet is not where the answer is.

## The unit that makes it visible

**One teaspoon of sugar is about 4 g, and about 16 calories.** Everything below is that number multiplied, because teaspoons are the one unit everyone in an Indian kitchen already has.

The guidance is consistent enough across bodies to be worth quoting: keep **free sugars under 10% of daily energy, and ideally under 5%**. On a 2,000-calorie day that second number is about **25 g — six teaspoons**. Free sugars means the sugar you add plus the sugar in juice, honey, jaggery and syrups. It does not include the sugar inside fruit or milk, which arrives with fibre, water and protein attached.

Six teaspoons is not a lot, and it is not what a normal day contains.

## A day that did not feel like a sugar day

| Where it came from | Sugar |
| --- | --- |
| Three cups of chai, two teaspoons each | 24 g |
| Four biscuits, with one of those cups | 8 g |
| One tablespoon of ketchup, with lunch | 4 g |
| One 200 ml packaged fruit drink | 20 g |
| Two teaspoons of a malt drink mix, evening | 6 g |
| **Total** | **about 62 g — fifteen teaspoons** |

And that day had no mithai in it, no cake, no ice cream. Add one piece of mithai at a family dinner and you are past 80 g — nearly three times the guidance — without a single meal having changed.

Which is the point: the meals were fine. The sugar was in the gaps between them.

## Where it hides

- **Chai and coffee.** Two teaspoons a cup, four times a day, is 32 g. It is the largest single line in most Indian days and the easiest one to change.
- **Biscuits.** They are not dessert; they are what happens next to a cup. Four biscuits is roughly two teaspoons.
- **Anything labelled fruit.** A fruit yoghurt, a fruit drink, a fruit cereal bar: the fruit is usually flavouring, and the sugar is on the label.
- **Sauces.** Ketchup, chilli sauce, sandwich spreads, some pickles. A tablespoon is a teaspoon of sugar.
- **Breakfast cereal and muesli.** Even the ones without frosting. "No added sugar" versions often carry the same total from dried fruit and juice concentrate.
- **Health drinks and malt mixes.** A product sold on the strength of its vitamins can carry more sugar per serving than the biscuits it is competing with.
- **Mithai, and the festival week around it.** Half a kilo of mithai arrives at most Indian homes at some point every year, and it is eaten as an obligation rather than a choice.

## How to read the label in ten seconds

The front of a packet is advertising and is regulated much more loosely than the back. The panel on the back is the part with rules, and four habits are enough to read it:

1. **Use the per 100 g column, never the per-serving column.** Serving sizes are chosen by the manufacturer, and they are often smaller than what a person eats. Per 100 g lets you compare two products at all.
2. **Look for "of which sugars".** Roughly: above 10 g per 100 g is a sweetened food, above 20 g per 100 g is a sweet. Below 5 g per 100 g is genuinely low.
3. **Read the first three ingredients.** Ingredients are ordered by weight. If sugar, glucose, syrup, dextrose, malt extract or fruit juice concentrate appears in the first three, the front of the pack is doing something the back does not support.
4. **Treat these as sugar too:** honey, jaggery, date syrup, fruit juice concentrate. They have the same calories and largely the same effect. Jaggery is not a remedy; it is sugar with minerals in it.

That is also the practical case for the barcode side of the app on [the features page](/features) — a scan that reads the panel and gives you the sugars per 100 g is faster than turning the packet around, and it does the part of label reading that people skip.

## What to change, in the order it pays

1. **Chai.** Two teaspoons down to one saves about 12 g a day, which is half the guidance in one decision you make four times a day. It takes about a week to stop noticing.
2. **The biscuit habit.** Keep the chai, replace the biscuit with peanuts, chana or nothing. This is the same swap the fibre post makes, and it fixes two things at once.
3. **Juice to fruit.** A glass of juice is three oranges with the fibre left out. Whole fruit is slower to eat, and the fibre is [what makes it hold you](/blog/fibre-in-an-indian-diet).
4. **The spoon in the curd, and the sauce.** Individually small, together about two teaspoons a day.
5. **Mithai as an occasion.** One piece, on the day, enjoyed. Half a box over a week is not a treat, it is a habit with a nicer name.

## Two things this post is not saying

**Fruit is not the problem.** Whole fruit carries sugar with fibre, water and micronutrients, it is difficult to overeat, and no guidance on this page is asking you to avoid it. Juice and dried fruit are the concentrated versions, and those are worth watching. [What a fruit bowl does and does not carry](/blog/fruit-and-the-fruit-bowl) is the longer version of that paragraph.

**And this is not a diabetes plan.** If you have diabetes, pre-diabetes, PCOS or a thyroid condition, the useful version of this conversation is with your doctor or dietitian — blood sugar is driven by more than sugar, and a general post full of teaspoons is not a treatment plan. If you have ever had a difficult relationship with food, a sugar audit is also not the exercise to start; that is worth saying plainly rather than at the end of a list.

Where the sugar is least of a problem is also where the label is least needed: a dish cooked at home from pulses and vegetables has no ingredient list to read. That is [the other advantage of home cooking](/blog/why-home-cooked-food-is-hard-to-track), and it is why the packaged-food problem gets worse exactly when cooking stops, which is [what eating out does to a week](/blog/eating-out-without-losing-the-plan).
