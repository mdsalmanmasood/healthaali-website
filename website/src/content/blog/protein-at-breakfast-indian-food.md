Most Indian breakfasts are good food. Poha with peanuts, two idlis and sambar, a dosa, a paratha with curd — these are fast, cheap, familiar, and they have fed people well for a long time. What they are not is protein-heavy, and almost nobody knows the actual numbers, because nobody has ever had a reason to look them up.

So look them up. This is the meal where the gap in an Indian day usually starts, and it is also the easiest one to close, because the same breakfast happens most days of the week.

## The arithmetic, dish by dish

| Breakfast as served | Calories | Protein |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 plate poha | about 250 | about 4 g |
| 1 plate upma | about 260 | about 5 g |
| 2 idli with a katori of sambar | about 200 | about 6 g |
| 1 masala dosa | about 380 | about 6 g |
| 2 plain parathas | about 350 | about 6 g |
| 2 slices of bread, butter and jam | about 250 | about 5 g |

For comparison, the things that carry protein:

| Addition | Protein |
| --- | --- |
| 2 eggs | about 12 g |
| 1 katori of curd | about 5 g |
| 30 g peanuts, with the poha | about 7 g |
| 50 g paneer | about 9 g |
| 2 moong dal chillas, or 2 pesarattu | about 11 g |
| 40 g oats with 200 ml milk | about 12 g |
| 50 g soya chunks (dry weight) | about 26 g |

Read those two tables together and the problem is obvious. A poha-and-chai breakfast is about 5 g of protein. If an adult needs somewhere between 55 and 90 g in a day — the arithmetic is in [how much protein you actually need](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet) — then lunch and dinner have to carry 25 to 40 g each, every day, which is not how most people eat. Breakfast is not "a meal you skipped protein at". It is the reason the target looks impossible.

## Why the first meal is worth starting with

Not because breakfast is magic, and not because skipping it is a crime. Three practical reasons:

- **It is decided the same way every day.** Whatever you eat at 8 am, you eat most mornings. Changing one habit changes two hundred and fifty meals a year, which is not true of any other meal.
- **It sets what happens at 11 am.** A 5 g breakfast is a hungry morning, and the hungry morning is what gets answered by chai and biscuits — [the day's other invisible calorie](/blog/sugar-in-an-ordinary-indian-day).
- **It is the cheapest place to add protein.** Adding 12 g at breakfast costs two eggs or a katori of curd. Adding 12 g at dinner usually means cooking a second dish.

## Three ways to fix it

**1. Add, do not replace.** Keep the poha. Put two eggs beside it, or a katori of curd, or a small bowl of sprouts, or a handful of peanuts stirred into the poha itself. Nothing about the breakfast you like has to change, and you have gone from 5 g to 17 g. This is the version we recommend first, because it survives a busy Tuesday.

**2. Make the base carry more.** Moong dal in the poha. More dal and less water in the sambar. Sprouts in the upma. A protein filling in the dosa instead of potato. A moong dal chilla instead of a maida one. Each of these is the same dish with a different balance rather than a new recipe to learn.

## What this is not

**It is not an argument that everyone must eat breakfast.** Plenty of people genuinely are not hungry at 8 am, and forcing food down to satisfy a target is a bad trade. If you skip breakfast, the arithmetic does not disappear — it moves to lunch and dinner, and 30 to 35 g in each of two meals is what it takes. That is doable; it just has to be deliberate.

**It is not a dietary prescription.** If you take medication that affects blood sugar, or you have a condition that changes how or when you should eat, the timing question belongs to your doctor. And nothing here is a reason to eat eggs or soya if you do not want to — the curd, the sprouts, the dal and the peanuts all work.

**And it is not a claim that poha is a bad breakfast.** It is an excellent one. It is simply a carbohydrate-led dish, and a carbohydrate-led breakfast at 8 am is why the day is short of protein at 8 pm. The fix is one addition, made once, repeated for a year.
