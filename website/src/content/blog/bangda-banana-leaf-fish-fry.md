There are two ways fish arrives on an Indian table: in a curry, or as a fry. The fry is the one that can quietly cost more than it looks, because the fish itself is the lean part — the batter, the coating and the oil around it are where an ordinary fry stops being a light dish.

This one is built the other way round. Bangda — Indian mackerel, as the channel's own description gives it — is wrapped in a banana leaf and cooked with what that same description calls minimal oil. It is the only banana leaf fish we have filmed, and the reason it works is worth understanding, because it is a technique before it is a recipe.

## What the leaf is doing

The banana leaf here is not a plate. It is the cooking surface, and it is doing two of the jobs that frying normally does.

A leaf wrapped around a piece of fish holds in the moisture the fish is already giving off, so the fish cooks in its own steam as much as against the heat — and it is the leaf, not the fish, that touches the pan. What that buys you is the texture people are actually after when they fry fish, from a pan that is barely oiled. What it costs you is the oil you did not spend. The description's "minimal oil" is a real instruction, and it is why this dish belongs in the less-oil half of the kitchen rather than in the fried half of it.

It is the same argument as [the post on cooking with less oil](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka): decide where the oil goes rather than trying to remove it from the kitchen. The leaf is simply one of the more elegant places to apply it.

## One portion, and the table it sits in

The number that makes the case for this dish is protein, and it is one this site has published before: **100 g of cooked bangda carries 19 to 22 g**. A palm-sized piece is about 100 g — that is the portion [the balanced thaali post](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali) works from — so one serving of this dish is carrying roughly what a whole ordinary lunch carries.

Read against the rest of the kitchen:

| What one serving looks like | Protein |
| --- | --- |
| Fish, such as bangda — 100 g cooked | 19 to 22 g |
| Paneer — 100 g | 18 to 20 g |
| Soya chunks — 30 g dry | about 15 g |
| Two whole eggs | about 12 g |
| One katori of thick dal | about 12 to 13 g |
| One katori of curd | about 5 to 6 g |

Every figure in that column is already on this site: [the comparison of the four vegetarian proteins](/blog/paneer-eggs-dal-soya-which-protein) has the per-serving version of the table, and [the protein post](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet) works through where a day's target comes from and why most Indian plates miss it.

One comparison is worth sitting with. A plate of two rotis, a katori of dal, a bowl of rice and a sabzi lands somewhere around 20 to 24 g of protein — and here one piece of fish, nothing else on the plate, is close to 20 g. That is what makes this a **high-protein lunch** rather than a smaller lunch: one component is deliberately protein, and the rest of the plate does not have to shrink for it. The version of "high protein" that survives a Tuesday is the one where a single dish does the work.

## Where fish sits in the week

For a kitchen that cooks non-veg, fish is usually the fastest of the options: a piece of fish is cooked through in minutes, where a chicken curry takes longer and mutton longer still. It is also the non-veg dish most often cooked as a fry, which is exactly why the leaf version is worth adding to the rotation — it keeps what people like about a fried fish and drops most of the oil.

If fish is not on your table, nothing in the arithmetic changes. Paneer, eggs, soya and dal sit in the same column above, and the plate logic below works with any of them.

## The plate it lands on

A fried fish is usually a side dish in a large spread. In this arithmetic it is the main event, which means the rest of the plate can stay simple — and, importantly, can stay at all.

- **Rice.** Fish and rice is the pairing the meal wants, and a katori of rice is about 130 calories with almost no fat in it. [The post on keeping rice](/blog/weight-loss-with-indian-food-without-banning-rice) makes that case properly; this is a dish that would be worse without it.
- **A vegetable, in a real portion.** The fish carries the protein; the sabzi carries the volume and the fibre. If fibre is the thing your own day is usually short of, [it has its own post](/blog/fibre-in-an-indian-diet).
- **Something cold at the side.** Curd, a salad, chaas — the part of [the balanced thaali](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali) that makes a meal feel finished rather than merely eaten.

And if the month's goal is to lose weight, this is the shape of lunch that makes it easier to hold: about 20 g of protein in the middle of the day is what keeps the late afternoon from being settled by packets, which is [the entire subject of the 5 pm post](/blog/high-protein-evening-snacks).

## What this post is not claiming

**Not a recipe.** The marinade, the leaf and the timing are in the video, where they belong. Nothing here is a step-by-step, and no ingredient table or calorie count is printed for this dish, because we have not measured it and will not invent numbers for it.

**Not a zero-oil dish.** The description says minimal oil, not none, and this site's position does not change for a fish recipe: a little oil in the right place is a good calorie, and the goal is deciding where it goes. [The less-oil post](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka) says all of this before it says anything else.

**Not an omega-3 supplement.** The description lists omega-3 among the reasons to cook it, and mackerel is an oily fish, which is where those fats sit — but we are not going to turn that into a daily figure, and one dish is not a dose of anything.

**Not advice for anyone managing a condition.** If you have gout, kidney disease, or anything else that changes how much protein or purine belongs in your day, that number is your doctor's to give, not a recipe page's.

The dish itself, with the channel's description in its own words, is on [the high-protein page](/high-protein-recipes) and [the no-oil page](/no-oil-recipes), and every dish we have filmed is on [the recipes page](/recipes).
