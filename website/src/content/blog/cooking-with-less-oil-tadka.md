We are not going to tell you oil is bad for you. Oil is fat, fat is a nutrient, and a meal with some fat in it is usually a better meal than one without. What we will say is that oil is the easiest thing in an Indian kitchen to spend without noticing, and that most of the cooking we film started as a test of whether a dish still tastes like itself with a fraction of it.

A teaspoon of oil is 5 ml. That is about **4.5 grams, which is about 40 calories**. Keep that number in your head for the rest of this post, because everything here is that number, multiplied.

## Where the oil goes

An ordinary day, in a kitchen that is not trying to be careful:

| Where | Oil | Calories |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sabzi for two, 2 tsp to start | 9 g | 80 |
| Tadka for the dal, 2 tsp | 9 g | 80 |
| Tawa cooking for breakfast, 1 to 2 tsp | 7 g | 60 |
| One deep-fried snack, a samosa | 8 to 12 g | 70 to 110 |

That is roughly **300 calories a day from oil alone**, before anyone has fried anything on purpose. Add one restaurant meal or one plate of pakoras and it can double.

None of these amounts is absurd. Each one is a normal spoon. That is exactly why the total is easy to miss: there is no single moment where you decide to eat 300 calories of oil. It accumulates in four small decisions.

## The vegetables are not the problem

Something we notice whenever we read a food log: the oil shows up on the plate, not in the pan. A bhindi fry made with two tablespoons of oil is a different food from the same bhindi made with one teaspoon and a splash of water — and the second one tastes like bhindi, which is the point.

The swap is not "eat boring food". It is "do not pay 200 calories for the texture of frying when the dish was never really fried". Two techniques do most of the work for us:

- **Roast the spices dry first.** Whole cumin, mustard seeds and dried chilli bloom perfectly well in a hot dry pan. You get the aroma you were after from the tempering, and you can add a teaspoon of oil at the end, where it contributes flavour and nothing else.
- **Give the onions water, not oil.** Onions soften because they lose water and their cell walls collapse, not because they are sitting in fat. A splash of water with the lid on does the same job in about the same time.

A third habit that matters more than either: **preheat properly**. Food that hits a lukewarm pan sticks, and the instinct when it sticks is to add more oil. A properly hot pan plus a well-seasoned or non-stick surface needs a fraction of what a cold one does.

Those techniques are general, and they behave differently in a dosa pan than in a kadai. [The pan-by-pan version](/blog/cooking-without-oil-pan-by-pan) goes through them dish by dish, including the one where we do not claim zero oil at all.

## What we do not claim

We are not saying zero-oil food is always better than a dish cooked with oil. A teaspoon of mustard oil poured over a finished dal, or a little ghee on a roti, is one of the best-value calories in Indian cooking: 40 calories of flavour that makes the rest of the plate more likely to be eaten.

We are also not saying frying is poisonous. A deep-fried snack once a week is not a problem. The problem, when there is one, is the invisible spoon in the middle of a normal Tuesday, and the fact that it does not register as a decision at all.

The honest version of this advice is: **decide where your oil goes rather than removing it.** Most of our recipes still use some, and it is deliberate.

## Try it on one dish

Changing the whole kitchen at once is how diets fail. Pick one dish you cook often — the daily sabzi, the tadka you make every morning, or fish night — and cut the oil by half using the pan and water techniques above. If it tastes worse, use three-quarters of the original next time and keep the change. If it tastes the same, you have just moved 100 to 150 calories a day without giving anything up, which is a bigger change than most people manage by willpower. ([The banana leaf fish fry](/blog/bangda-banana-leaf-fish-fry) is the same principle taken further: the leaf takes the heat, so the pan needs almost none.)
