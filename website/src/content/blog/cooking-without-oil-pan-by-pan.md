The general advice — use less oil — is easy to agree with and hard to act on, because oil is not one thing in a kitchen. It is a different decision in a dosa pan, a sabzi kadai, a pressure cooker and a tawa, and a rule that ignores that is a rule you will abandon in a week.

So this is the same argument as [where the oil actually hides](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka), organised by what is in the pan. For each dish: what does the work instead, and where the honest answer is that something still needs fat.

## What replaces oil, in general

Four things do the work, and every dish below is a combination of them:

- **Heat.** A properly hot pan is the single biggest one, because the reflex when food sticks is to add oil. Food that hits a lukewarm pan sticks; food that hits a hot one often does not need anything under it.
- **Water.** Onions soften because they lose water and their walls collapse, not because they are sitting in fat. A splash and a lid does the same job in about the same time.
- **Dry roasting.** Whole cumin, mustard seeds and dried chilli bloom perfectly well in a hot dry pan. A teaspoon of oil added at the end then contributes flavour rather than heat transfer.
- **Steam and pressure.** Anything that can be steamed or pressure cooked is finished before the question of oil comes up.

## Roti and chapati

This is the easy one, and worth stating plainly because it is where most households spend oil without thinking: **a roti needs nothing on the tawa.** The dough has no fat in it, the tawa is hot, and the roti puffs.

What happens afterwards is the actual decision. A teaspoon of ghee on a finished roti is about 40 calories and one of the best-value spends in an Indian kitchen — it makes the rest of the plate more likely to be eaten. We do it. We just do it deliberately rather than as part of the cooking.

## Sabzi

The technique that matters here is the one the oil was standing in for:

1. **Get the pan hot before anything wet goes in.** Cold pan, wet vegetables, and you are now steaming something you meant to fry.
2. **Give the onions water, not oil.** Splash, lid, a few minutes. They will taste the same.
3. **Let the vegetables release their own water, then let it go.** Most vegetables are mostly water to begin with. Cook them until that water has evaporated rather than adding a liquid that then has to be dealt with.
4. **Dry roast the spices first**, then add them to the vegetables. You get the aroma a tadka was there to provide.

A bhindi fry made with a teaspoon and a splash of water is a different food from the same bhindi made with two tablespoons, and it tastes like bhindi. That is the point — you are not paying 200 calories for the texture of frying when the dish was not really fried.

## Dal, and the tadka

The tempering is where the oil goes in most Indian kitchens, and it is the easiest place to cut in half without changing the dish: **2 tsp to the tadka** is what an ordinary day spends, and both of those are gone before the dal is tasted.

Dry-roast the whole spices first. Then decide how much oil the finish is worth — a teaspoon poured over the finished dal carries flavour through the bowl, and it is being spent where you can taste it rather than where you cannot.

## Dosa, uttapam and cheela: the honest exception

This is where we refuse the tidy version of the advice. **A dosa on a bare pan does not work.** The batter needs a film to crisp and release, and a dosa cooked without one is a pale, torn version of the dish that nobody in the house will ask for twice.

Our answer is not zero oil; it is a much smaller film:

- Heat the pan properly, then wipe 1/4 tsp over it with kitchen paper — about 1 g, which is about 10 calories, against the tablespoon or more a restaurant pan uses.
- Use the smallest pan the batter fits, so the film you do spend is spread over the batter rather than over the empty pan.
- A well-seasoned cast-iron or a good non-stick tawa needs less than a worn one, and a hot one needs less than a cool one.

The same applies to a cheela, with one advantage: a batter with soya, oats or besan in it holds together better than a plain one, so it needs less encouragement to leave the pan. That is [the trick the soya post is about](/blog/soya-chunks-cheapest-protein).

## The three places fat is still worth spending

1. **The finished dal.** A teaspoon of mustard oil poured over a hot dal is 40 calories of flavour that makes the whole bowl better.
2. **The roti.** See above. Ghee on a roti is not a failure of the plan; it is the plan working.
3. **Anything actually fried.** A samosa is not a dry-pan dish and pretending otherwise is how people end up making a worse version of the thing they wanted.

## What we do not claim

We are not saying oil is bad, that fat is not a nutrient, or that a zero-oil dish is always better than one cooked with oil. Most of the dishes we film still use some, deliberately. The problem — when there is one — is the invisible spoon in the middle of a normal Tuesday, and the fact that it never registers as a decision at all.

The version of this that works is [one dish at a time](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka): pick the sabzi you cook every day, halve the oil, and keep the change if it tastes the same. That is 100 to 150 calories a day, which is more than most people manage by giving up something they like — and [where it sits in the rest of the plate](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali) is a separate question.
