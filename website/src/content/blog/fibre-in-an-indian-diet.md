Fibre is the nutrient nobody counts. Protein gets the labels, calories get the apps, and fibre gets a sentence at the end of an article telling you to eat more salad. Then the salad sits next to the plate uneaten, and the day ends 15 g short of what it needed.

That is worth fixing, because fibre is the thing that makes a lot of the rest of it easier: it is what decides whether a meal holds you for four hours or two, it is most of what makes pulses and vegetables filling for the calories they carry, and it is the one number that quietly improves when you eat the food this site is about.

## What the target actually is

Recommendations are not unanimous, which is normal for nutrition and worth saying out loud rather than quoting one figure as if it were a law. Indian dietary guidance sits near **40 g a day for an adult on about 2,000 calories**. Most Western guidance says **25 to 30 g**. Both are describing the same thing: pulses, vegetables, whole fruit and whole grains at most meals rather than at one.

The practical version, then, is not a target. It is a check: if your day has one pulse, one vegetable, no raw salad and no whole fruit, you are somewhere near 15 g, and no amount of reading will change that. If it has two pulses, real vegetable portions and fruit you actually ate, you are near 30 g without trying.

## Where fibre actually is

These are the portions as they land on a plate, not per 100 g of anything:

| On the plate | Fibre |
| --- | --- |
| 1 katori (150 ml) thick dal | about 7 g |
| 1 katori rajma or chana | about 6 g |
| 2 whole-wheat rotis | about 6 g |
| 1 katori sabzi | about 3 g |
| 1 katori raw salad | about 2 g |
| 1 apple, or 100 g guava | about 4 to 5 g |
| 30 g almonds or peanuts | about 3 g |
| 1 tablespoon chia or flax | about 3 to 4 g |

Two things stand out. The pulse is the single biggest contributor on an Indian plate, which is convenient, because it is also where the protein is. And the vegetable everyone treats as decoration — the cucumber-onion-tomato at the side — is worth about as much as half a katori of sabzi, if it gets eaten.

## The same day, both ways

This is where the gap shows up, and it is not about exotic food. Here is an ordinary day counted twice.

**The version that lands short:**

| Meal | Fibre |
| --- | --- |
| Breakfast: one plate poha | about 2 g |
| Lunch: 1 katori dal, 2 rotis, no sabzi | about 12 g |
| Evening: chai and four biscuits | about 1 g |
| Dinner: 1 katori dal, 2 rotis | about 11 g |
| **Total** | **about 26 g** |

**The same day with three decisions changed:**

| Meal | Fibre |
| --- | --- |
| Breakfast: the same poha, plus a guava | about 7 g |
| Lunch: dal, 2 rotis, 1 katori sabzi, salad | about 18 g |
| Evening: the same chai, plus a handful of peanuts | about 4 g |
| Dinner: rajma instead of dal, 2 rotis, sabzi | about 15 g |
| **Total** | **about 44 g** |

Same kitchen, same dishes, same effort. The difference is a vegetable, a fruit, a nut and a pulse swap — not a new diet. This is also why we keep saying that the plate matters more than the recipe, which is the whole argument of [how to build a balanced thaali](/blog/how-to-build-a-balanced-thaali).

## The four additions

In the order they are worth doing:

1. **A raw salad you actually eat.** Not a garnish, not two slices of tomato. A katori of cucumber, onion, tomato, carrot, with lemon and salt, at lunch and dinner. It is the cheapest fibre in the kitchen and it needs no cooking.
2. **A pulse at two meals, in a real katori.** Dal at lunch and rajma at dinner is about 13 g of fibre and about 18 g of protein. This is the single largest change available to an Indian day.
3. **Whole fruit instead of juice.** A glass of orange juice is the fibre of three oranges with the fibre left out. And where the skin is edible, keep it — cucumber, apple and guava carry a good part of their fibre there.
4. **Something whole, and a spoonful of seeds.** Atta rather than maida where the dish allows it, and a tablespoon of chia or flax stirred into curd, oats or a smoothie you were already making. Four grams, no new meal.

Notice that none of these is a product. Fibre is one of the few nutrients where the food version is straightforwardly better than the packaged version: a pulse brings water, protein and volume along with the fibre, which is exactly why it fills you up. A fibre-enriched biscuit brings the fibre and keeps the biscuit.

## Adding it without the discomfort

Fibre has a failure mode, and pretending otherwise is how people conclude that "fibre does not suit me".

- **Go up slowly.** Add one item a week, not all four at once. A sudden jump from 15 g to 40 g will produce bloating, and it is the food that gets blamed for it.
- **Drink water with it.** Fibre without fluid is what makes a stool harder rather than easier. If you increase one, increase the other.
- **Spread it across the day.** Thirty grams at dinner is a different experience from ten grams at three meals.
- **Give it two weeks.** The gut adjusts, and the first week is not the verdict.

## When this is the wrong conversation

Fibre is not universally good advice, and the exceptions are not small print. If you have irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, a stricture, or you are recovering from gut surgery, high-fibre eating can make symptoms worse, and what helps is a specific plan from a doctor or dietitian rather than a general post. The same goes for anyone whose problem is not what they eat but how much — if you are undereating, adding salad is not the fix.

For everyone else, the honest summary is this: fibre is the one target on this site you are most likely to hit by accident, once the plate has a pulse, a vegetable and a piece of fruit on it.
