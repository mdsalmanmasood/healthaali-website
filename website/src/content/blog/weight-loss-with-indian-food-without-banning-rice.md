There are two ways we see this go wrong, and they look like opposites.

The first is the crash: rice out, roti out, everything boiled, results for three weeks, and then a normal family dinner at the second month mark and it all comes back. The second is quieter and more common — eating "healthy" for two months with nothing to show for it, because the food improved and the quantity did not change. [Four explanations worth checking](/blog/eating-healthy-not-losing-weight) is the post that second failure mode turned into.

Both come from the same place: changing the wrong thing first. So here is the order we would work in.

## The mechanism, without the jargon

Weight comes down when you consistently eat slightly less energy than you use. That is the whole mechanism, and no food is exempt from it — not rice, not ghee, not fruit. Everything else (which foods keep you full, which ones you can sustain, how the cooking fits your week) matters because it decides whether you can hold the change, not because it changes the arithmetic.

The part that is not arithmetic: how much you need per day depends on your body, your activity and your medical situation. If you have diabetes, thyroid issues, PCOS, are pregnant, or take medication, this is a conversation with your doctor before it is a conversation with a food blog. Nothing on this page is medical advice.

## Change these three first

**1. The oil.** It is the largest source of calories in Indian home cooking that adds no volume, no protein and no fibre. A normal day carries around 300 calories of it without anyone frying anything on purpose — we go through the numbers in [where the oil actually hides](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka). Cutting that in half is roughly 150 calories a day, which is often more than people achieve by giving up rice entirely.

**2. The protein.** Higher protein is the change that makes an ordinary deficit tolerable, because it is what keeps you full. Most Indian plates land around 20 g a meal when nobody is paying attention; pushing it to 30 to 40 g is entirely doable with dal, curd, paneer, eggs, soya, fish or chicken, and it changes how an afternoon feels. [The protein post](/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-indian-diet) has the per-portion numbers.

**3. Drinks and snacks.** Not because they are uniquely fattening, but because they are the easiest place to spend 300 calories and forget them: sweet tea through the day, a packaged juice, biscuits with the evening chai. Two small changes here often beat a change made at dinner.

Only after those three would we look at the grain portion, and even then as a measured portion rather than a ban.

## Why we do not cut rice

Rice is about 130 calories a katori. It has almost no fat, it pairs with dal in a way that genuinely improves the protein picture, and it is the thing that makes a meal feel like a meal in most Indian homes. Removing it saves a moderate number of calories and costs a lot of compliance.

What usually happens when people cut rice is that the calories come back somewhere else — more of the fry, a bigger snack at 6 pm, a "healthy" replacement that costs more than the rice did. The swap that works is not rice to nothing; it is **two fists of rice to one**, decided at the serving spoon.

## Snacks decide more than dinner

The single most reliable pattern in a food log is a good day undone between 5 and 8 in the evening. Not by a big meal — by a packet, twice. That hour gets [its own post](/blog/high-protein-evening-snacks), because it fails for a different reason than dinner does.

The fix is not willpower. It is having something on hand that is genuinely satisfying, which in practice means protein and fibre rather than sugar and salt. A boiled chana chaat with onion, tomato and lemon does that job in about five minutes, and it is the snack in the video below.

## How fast, and how to know

Rates that are commonly given as safe and sustainable for most adults sit around **a quarter to half a kilogram a week**. Faster is not automatically better; it is usually water, and it is usually followed by the rebound that makes people give up. If you have only a few kilograms to lose, expect it to move more slowly than that, and expect it to look like nothing for two or three weeks at a time.

Weigh yourself in the same conditions once or twice a week and look at the **month**, not the day: salt, sleep and hormones move the number by a kilogram either way. A tape measure at the waist and how a shirt fits are both less noisy than the scale.

## The boring part

Everything above is unglamorous, and that is deliberate. The version of this that works is not the one that is best on paper — it is the one still running in week nine, on a day you are tired and someone else is cooking. Small changes to oil, protein, snacks and the rice portion are achievable in a real kitchen. A diet that requires a separate meal every night for the household is not.
