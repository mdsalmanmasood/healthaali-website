There is a pattern in almost every food log we have looked at, and it is not at dinner. It is between five and eight in the evening, and it is not one large meal — it is a packet, twice, eaten standing up.

Nobody decides to do this. It happens because the food that is ready at 5 pm is the food that comes in a wrapper, and the food that would help takes twenty minutes nobody has at that hour. So the useful question is not willpower. It is: **what can be on a plate in under five minutes, that carries protein?**

Here are the answers we actually use, with the arithmetic.

## The five-minute shelf

| What | Protein | Active time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A katori of curd, with salt, cumin and whatever vegetable is in the fridge | about 5 to 6 g | none |
| A katori of boiled chana or rajma, with onion, tomato, lemon, chaat masala | about 9 g | two minutes, if the batch exists |
| Two boiled eggs, salt and chilli | about 12 g | none, if boiled earlier |
| Paneer, cubed or grated, with pepper | about 18 g per 100 g | none |
| Roasted chana or peanuts, a small handful | depends on the handful | none |
| A wedge of leftover dal, heated, in a cup | about 9 to 12 g | one minute |

Read the third column again, because that is the whole post. **Every row that says "none" is a row that assumes something was done earlier**: eggs boiled on Sunday, a pot of chana cooked in the week, curd set. That is the actual change — not discipline at 5 pm, but ten minutes on a day when you have them.

## Why the protein matters more here than at dinner

A snack built on sugar and salt does one thing well: it makes you want another one. Protein and fibre do the opposite, which is why the chana option works better than the biscuit option even when the calories are similar.

The same argument runs through the rest of the day. Protein at breakfast is [the cheapest place to add it](/blog/protein-at-breakfast-indian-food), and the evening is where [the day's invisible calories](/blog/sugar-in-an-ordinary-indian-day) usually live — two teaspoons in each cup of chai, four times, is 32 g of sugar before anything has been called a snack.

So the evening hour is worth solving twice over: it replaces the packet, and it stops the 9 pm dinner from being a bigger one. That is the entire case for the high-protein evening snack as a category — it is not a treat being allowed, it is the plate that keeps the next meal a normal size.

## The chaat shape, which is the trick

The dish that solves this best in an Indian kitchen is a chaat, and the reason is structural rather than nutritional. It is assembled rather than cooked: a pulse carries the protein, raw vegetables carry the volume, lemon and chaat masala carry the flavour, and **nothing in it needs oil**. It keeps two days in the fridge and it is better cold than warm, which is exactly what a 5 pm plate needs.

The same assembly logic covers the other cold options: curd with grated cucumber and roasted cumin, or hung curd with salt, pepper and a little grated carrot. Both are done before the kettle boils.

## What to buy, and what not to

Packets are not the enemy — the enemy is a packet being the only thing within reach. Two categories are worth buying rather than cooking:

- **Roasted chana and peanuts.** Protein and fibre, no preparation, and they keep for months. A small bowl is a snack; the bag is not a serving, which is why we decant rather than eat from it.
- **Frozen or tinned pulses** if the soaked-and-cooked version never happens in your week. A tin of chickpeas at 5 pm beats a plan that requires Sunday.

And the one worth deliberately not buying: anything whose front of pack is a health claim and whose third ingredient is sugar. [The label post](/blog/sugar-in-an-ordinary-indian-day) goes through how that usually reads.

## What we do not claim

A snack is not a meal and this is not a plan. The numbers above are portions, not targets, and the protein in a katori of chana depends on what is in the katori — [the same water problem that moves the dal number](/blog/how-much-protein-in-dal). If the evening hour is where a day keeps failing, though, it is worth more attention than the meal everyone is already careful about, because it is the one nobody is watching.
