The objection to a fish fry without oil is always the same word: crisp. A fry is supposed to crackle, and oil is what crackles — so a fish cooked with none should be a pale compromise, steamed where it should have been fried.

The five-minute video this post is about disagrees, and it is worth taking seriously, because the crisp in a fry was never entirely the oil's work. Heat crisps. A coating crisps. Oil is the medium those two usually meet in, not the thing doing the crisping — and once you see it that way, a dry hot pan stops looking like a handicap.

## What is actually doing the work

Three things, none of them oil:

1. **A hot surface before the fish touches it.** The reflex when fish sticks is to add oil; the reason it stuck is usually that the pan was not hot enough. Fish hitting a properly hot pan sears instead of bonding to it.
2. **The coating.** Whatever coats the fish — the spiced marinade in the video — dries and crisps against the heat. A wet coating on a lukewarm pan steams; the same coating on a hot one fries in everything but the fat.
3. **Not crowding the pan.** The [pan-by-pan post](/blog/cooking-without-oil-pan-by-pan) says this about sabzi and it is twice as true for fish: pieces laid edge to edge release water faster than the heat can drive it off, and you are steaming again.

None of this is new equipment or a special ingredient. It is the ordinary fry with the oil taken out and the heat put in its place — which is also why it takes five minutes rather than twenty. There is nothing to heat a kadai of oil for, nothing to cool, nothing to strain and store.

## The calories that never happened

This site's standing number is that a teaspoon of oil is about 40 calories, and an ordinary shallow fry spends several tablespoons before anything is eaten. A fish fry for two can easily carry 300 calories of oil that nobody tasted as oil — it registered as "crisp", which the heat was providing anyway.

That is the whole argument for the zero-oil version over the ordinary one. Not that oil is bad — [we do not say that anywhere](/blog/cooking-with-less-oil-tadka) — but that 300 calories spent where they cannot be tasted is a bad purchase, and here the same crisp is available without spending them.

## Against the banana-leaf fry

This kitchen now has two answers to the same craving: the [bangda banana-leaf fry](/blog/bangda-banana-leaf-fish-fry), which uses minimal oil and the leaf as its cooking surface, and this one, which uses none and a hot pan. They are not rivals. The leaf version is the better dish when the fish needs gentle cooking and the meal wants aroma; the dry-pan version is the faster one, and the one for an evening when the question is simply whether a fry can happen on a Tuesday.

Both belong to the same week. Fish cooks through in minutes either way, which is what makes it the fastest non-veg option in this kitchen — and the protein arithmetic is identical: a palm-sized piece carries roughly 20 g, which is what makes either version a main dish rather than a side.

## What this post is not claiming

**Not that every fry can go zero-oil.** A samosa is not a dry-pan dish and neither is anything that needs depth-frying; pretending otherwise produces a worse version of the thing you wanted. This works for fish because fish is thin, fast, and happy against direct heat.

**Not a measured calorie count.** We have not measured this dish and will not invent numbers for it. The 300-calorie figure above is the oil an ordinary fry spends, which is a property of tablespoons, not of this recipe.

**Not advice for anyone managing a condition.** Fish is protein-dense and some preparations are high in sodium; if a doctor has given you numbers, theirs outrank a recipe page's.
